# Proposal

A proposal tells a customer, in plain words, what they asked for, what will be done, what it
costs, how long it takes and on what terms. It is written for one customer after a call.

## Parts, in this order

1. **What you told us**: the customer's need in their own words, in a few lines. This proves
   we listened. Use what they said on the call, not what we wish they had said.
2. **What we will do**: the work, step by step, in plain words. One short line a step.
3. **What it costs**: each item with its price, then the total. Prices come only from the
   company's price file. If an item is not in that file, write "to be confirmed".
4. **How long it takes**: the days or weeks for each step, and the day work could begin.
5. **Our terms**: when payment is due, how long this proposal holds, and any deposit. Only
   what the company's terms file says.

## Length and shape

- One to two pages. Short lines and short sentences.
- A heading for each part, in the order above, and no other headings.
- A greeting by name at the top and a short closing line saying what happens next.
- Write "you" for the customer and "we" for the company.

## What a good one has

- The customer can see their own words in the first part.
- Every number in the cost part can be traced to a price file or a quotation.
- The next step is one clear action, such as replying to confirm.

## What to avoid

- No price, date or promise that is not in the company's files or on the call.
- No long lists of features the customer did not ask for.
- No pressure and no made-up deadlines.
- Do not call the formal quotation separate work for the owner. If it is mentioned, say the Bookkeeper can draft it from this proposal and the owner approves it.
- No sending. This is a draft for the owner to read and send themselves.

## Before you hand it over

- Read it once more, start to finish, as the person who will receive it.
- Say plainly at the top if anything was unclear or missing.
- Mark every line that came from a guess, so the owner can check it.
