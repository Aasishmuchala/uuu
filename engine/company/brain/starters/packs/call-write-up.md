# Call write-up

A call write-up turns a call or a meeting into one page the team can act on. It is written
from the notes or the transcript and from nothing else.

## Parts, in this order

Use these names as the headings, in this order, and no other headings.

1. **What they told us**: the background they gave, in a few short lines. Facts about their place, their need or their situation.
2. **Decisions**: what was agreed, one line each. Only what was said out loud.
3. **Who owns what**: each job with one name beside it. If no one was named, write "not decided".
4. **Deadlines**: each date that was said, with what it is for. Write dates in full.
5. **Risks**: what could go wrong, as the people on the call described it. If nothing was said, write "Nothing was said."
6. **What they asked for**: the other side's requests, in their own words where possible. Background goes in part 1, not here.
7. **Open questions**: what was left hanging.

## Length and shape

- One page. Aim for a page a person reads in two minutes.
- Short lines. One idea to a line. No paragraph longer than three lines.
- Begin with one sentence saying who was on the call and what it was about.
- Use the names as they were spoken. Do not shorten or guess a name.

## What a good one has

- Every decision can be found without reading the rest.
- Every owner is a person, never "the team".
- Every deadline has a day, or says "no date given".
- The open questions come last.

## What to avoid

- Nothing that was not said. Do not fill a gap with a guess; write "not said".
- No prices or promises unless they were spoken on the call, and then quote them as spoken.
- No opinions about the people on the call.
- No sending. The write-up is a draft for the owner to read first.

## Before you hand it over

- Read each decision against the notes once more.
- Check that every owner and every date appears in the notes.
- Say plainly at the top if the notes were thin or unclear.
- Mark every line that came from a guess, so the owner can check it.
